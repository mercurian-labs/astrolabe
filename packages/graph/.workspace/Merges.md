# Merges

A merge is a thread taking context from another thread — positionally, into where you stand. You are in one thread; you pick a checkpoint on another; your next message carries what that thread learned or did since the two diverged, compacted, into your thread's next turn. **Conversation and outcomes arrive as compacted context; the two threads stay two threads.** _(Amended 2026-09-24, with the thread-as-one-line model on [[Threads]]: formerly a merge folded a branch of one thread's history into another as a commit with two parents. The act, its direction, and its stroke survive; what changed is recorded below.)_

## The design (canonical)

- A merge is a human act — "forks and transfers are human-driven only" ([[DAG]]) — and it is **positional**: you take into where you stand. There is no choosing which thread carries; the thread you are in carries because you are in it. The act is picking a **checkpoint on another thread** and taking it in: the take stages in the [[Composer]], and your next send lands it — the message and what it took enter the turn together, and the turn's checkpoint records the transfer. Staging is draft-local until the send, like everything else in the composer ("nothing exists until its first commit").
- What arrives is the **delta**: what the source thread learned and did between where the two diverged — a fork's origin checkpoint, otherwise the latest checkpoint both descend from, or the source's whole history when they share none — and the picked checkpoint. Decisions, files changed, commands and tests run, conclusions, unresolved issues; never protocol noise, never the transcript whole. The delta is **compacted**, and the compaction is an artifact of the record — inspectable in the checkpoint's popover, regenerable, superseded when a later take supersedes it — never hidden prompt text. The assistant's responding turn is what receives it; the act — where you stand, which checkpoint you take — is yours.
- The continuation is simply your thread continuing. The merge never summarizes the thread you stand on — it weighs after the merge what it weighed before, plus the compaction — and the source thread is **untouched, not frozen**: still its own, still forkable, still able to take from yours later.
- On the [[Checkpoint Graph]] the asymmetry draws itself: your thread's line stays solid, and a **dashed edge** arrives from the taken checkpoint — an instance of the product-wide grammar that a dashed line means compacted context ([[Visual Language]]).
- A merge records the [[Providers|provider and model]] its responding turn runs under, like any turn-opening commit — the choice in effect at your thread's tip, switchable in the [[Composer]] before you send (the 2026-08 amendment on [[Assistant]]). The compaction runs under that choice.
- You can take across visibility — a published thread's checkpoint into a private thread — and the transfer stays private until you publish; publishing pulls the source's ancestry up to the taken checkpoint along ([[Publishing]]).
- A thread may take from several threads over time, each its own transfer with its own compaction; whether one send may take several checkpoints at once is a composer choice, not a model one (the n-ary resolution below).

**Amended (2026-08-28): compaction narrows to the folded parents.** As first designed, a merge compacted _both_ parent threads and started a new "evolution" — continuation "almost fresh, without rotting context." That default made every merge cost a healthy thread: the line you were actively working degraded to a summary because you brought another line home. Now a merge costs only what taking in costs. Given up: the almost-fresh restart. Lightening the continuing thread was never the merge's job; a fresh fork remains the way to travel light. _(2026-09-24: holds verbatim under the transfer — only the taken side is compacted, ever.)_

**Amended (2026-08-28, later the same day): the merge is positional — you fold into where you stand.** The first pass of the amendment above kept a merge-time choice of which parent carries, seeded and switchable. That picker is gone: standing in your thread _is_ the choice, and what you pick is the other thread's checkpoint. The reframing also gained precision — you take a thread as of any of its checkpoints, not only its tip — and it settled where the act lives: staged in the composer, landed by your next send. Given up: the symmetric reunion framing, and the picker with it. _(2026-09-24: holds; the transfer is positional by construction — it has a source and a taker, never two parents.)_

**Amended (2026-09-24): a merge is a transfer of context, not a commit with two parents.** Under the thread-as-one-line model a thread's history cannot hold a second parent, and the orchestrator the product sits on already carries exactly this act: a delta from a fork's origin to a later checkpoint, built when the next message on the taking thread needs it and injected with it, recorded as an inspectable artifact rather than as prompt text ([[Strategy]]). Rather than build a merge commit beside it, the design adopts the transfer and keeps everything the merge already meant: the human act, the positional direction, the compaction of the taken side only, the dashed stroke. Given up: the folded thread being _superseded_ — a taken thread is untouched, and the record of the two threads stays two records; the tree-style rendering contract, which drew a merge under each of its parents — a transfer has no parents to draw under; and code arriving with the fold, which is the reopened decision below.

_(An amendment of 2026-09-04 that widened "what merges is the plan" to project documents, and its 2026-09-16 simplification when those documents retired, are superseded by this: what a merge carries is the delta above.)_

## What exists today

The adopted orchestrator (2026-09-24) carries taking a fork's context back into its source — the delta built lazily and injected with the next message on the source thread. Mercurian's staging in the composer, the dashed edge, the popover's compaction, taking from a thread that is not one's own fork, and the code question below are not built.

## Open Decisions

### What does a merge do to the working state?

Under [[Threads]], a line of history can carry changed files — worktree state per repository, checkpointed turn by turn. Folding a line in brings its conversation as a compacted summary, but code cannot arrive as summary: files either merge or they don't. Three ways through:

- **Git merge into the current line's working state, performed by the responding turn.** The merge's responding turn git-merges the folded node's checkpointed state into each affected repository's worktree — the current line's worktree is the merge site; no new worktree, no new git branch. Conflicts are the agent's to resolve inside the turn, under the composer's runtime mode like any working-tree action, and the result lands checkpointed as the turn's reviewable diff — with the graph as the rewind if the reconciliation goes wrong. A repository only the folded line touched simply adopts its state. The dashed grammar stays honest: dashed marks the conversation that arrived as summary; the code arrives whole, because a git merge loses nothing.
- **A fresh third branch and worktree merging both.** Symmetric machinery under an asymmetric act: it contradicts merge-from-where-you-stand, multiplies worktrees, and leaves ambiguous which working state is the thread's.
- **Code doesn't merge.** Folding is conversation only; each line's code leaves by its own git exits. Cheapest, but a half-unification: the record reunifies while the build stays forked.

**Recommendation:** the first — the merge is one act with one site, the current line's worktree, and the responding turn does both reconciliations (compaction and files) under the same recorded model choice and runtime mode. Raised rather than resolved because it decides real machinery: conflict handling inside a turn, and what a merge means for a repository whose worktree hasn't materialized on the current line.

**Resolved (2026-08-28): git merge into the carrying line's working state, performed by the responding turn — as recommended.** The merge is one act with one site: the responding turn git-merges the folded line's branch, as of the picked node, into the current line's worktree per affected repository — a repository the carrying line never edited materializes at the merge, its first edit, and a repository only the carrying line touched is untouched. Conflicts are the agent's to resolve inside the turn, under the composer's runtime mode like any working-tree action, and the result lands checkpointed as the turn's reviewable diff, with the graph as the rewind if the reconciliation goes wrong. Given up: the cheapness of not merging code, and any symmetric third worktree. Accepted: conflict resolution happens inside a turn, which is new machinery — but it is working-tree work like any other, and the runtime mode already governs exactly that.

**Reopened (2026-09-24): the transfer moves context, not files.** Each thread has its own branches, and the delta _describes_ the files the source changed without moving any. Three ways through again. **The taking turn merges the code as ordinary work:** the compaction names the source thread's branch and what changed on it, and the agent git-merges or cherry-picks it into its own working tree inside the turn, under the runtime mode, landing as the turn's reviewable diff — the 2026-08-28 resolution, now the agent's act rather than the product's. **Code doesn't travel:** the delta tells the agent what the other thread built, and it rebuilds or leaves it, as it would with any teammate's branch. **The product merges before the turn:** a git merge of the source branch into the taking thread's worktree performed by the product as part of the act. **Recommendation:** the first. The product's rule that it never commits to a thread's branch on its own ([[Threads]]) holds; the runtime mode already governs working-tree actions; and a merge the agent performs is reviewable as the turn's diff and undoable by fork. What it gives up: a guarantee that taking context also takes code — a turn under a supervised mode may decline the merge, and the record must say so plainly.

### Binary or n-ary merges?

Can a merge have exactly two parents, or any number? **Binary** is simpler to select, review, and render, and pairwise reconciliation is easier for a human to check — but unifying N finalized parts takes N−1 sequential merges, each with its own compaction, so summaries degrade (compaction of compactions) and the graph fills with partial unifications nobody asked for. **N-ary** matches the actual use case — work in N parts, unify once — with a single compaction pass and one legible reunification event per merge, at the cost of a busier merge UI and harder review of an N-way reconciliation. **Recommendation:** model merges as n-ary — that's the hard-to-reverse choice — and let the first UI restrict selection to two if N-way review proves hard in practice.

**Resolved (2026-07): n-ary, as recommended.** The data model carries the real use case, and the first UI may restrict selection to two. Given up: nothing at the model level; the N-way review burden is accepted as a UI problem, not a model one. _(2026-08-28: with compaction narrowed to the folded parents, sequential folds no longer degrade anything, since the current line is never compacted, so the pressure that motivated n-ary is largely gone. 2026-09-24: under the transfer the question dissolves — a thread takes from any number of threads over time, each take its own transfer with its own compaction, and none compacts the taker; whether one send may stage several takes is the composer's choice.)_
