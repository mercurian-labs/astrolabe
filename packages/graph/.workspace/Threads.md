# Threads

A thread is the unit of work: one conversation, one line of checkpoints, and the working state to research and build. **Conversation and building happen in the same thread.** A thread works on the project's code across every repository it links and on the project's [[Memory|memory]]; whatever files an agent writes along the way — Markdown included — are ordinary files in those repositories, and the product does not distinguish them by genre. _(2026-09-16: the durable plan and spec documents this note once said threads worked on retired with the plan-first ruling on [[Mercurian]].)_

**A thread never branches inside itself.** Its history is a single line: each turn settles into one checkpoint after the one before it, and nothing in a thread has two parents. Divergence lives one level up — continuing from an earlier checkpoint **forks a new thread**, and a project's threads, with the forks and context transfers that relate them, form its [[DAG]]. Bringing work back together is likewise between threads: a thread **takes context from another thread**, which is what a [[Merges|merge]] now is. Every thread knows its parent, what made it (a fork you opened, or a child an agent spawned — [[DAG]]), the checkpoint it started from, and the root of its family. _(Amended 2026-09-24; the record is under "History: one line per thread" below. Where older passages on this note say *line*, read *thread*.)_

**A thread has one human owner and one agent runtime at a time.** The human is the thread's owner; pairing is forking, never co-owning ([[Collaborative DAG]]). The agent is whichever provider the thread's current turn runs under — switchable between turns as a recorded handoff ([[Assistant]]), never two at once — and a child thread an agent spawns is that agent's own ([[DAG]]). Several agents for one person means several threads, each in its own working state. Inside a thread, then, there are exactly two peers on one working state: the agent writing files in the slot, and you writing the same files through the [[Atlas Editor|editor]] — and an edit you make while a turn is running reaches that turn as a steer ("The working state", below). _(2026-09-24.)_

Its contained concepts are [[Composer]], [[Thread Tabs]].

## Born on the first commit

A thread exists once its history does. Born blank, it is a draft — purely local, nothing beyond your composer — until your first message lands as the root commit and makes it real. One rule: **nothing exists until its first commit** — a thread you never messaged never existed, and no empty rows sit in the sidebar. The same rule reaches the working state below: a worktree you never worked in never existed either.

_(2026-09-16: the second birth — real at import, with a spec derived from a tracker issue as the root commit — retired with issue import and the plan-first ruling on [[Mercurian]]; whether a thread can start from a tracker issue at all is open there.)_

A fork is born the same way. Forking records where the new thread comes from and nothing else — no assistant session, no worktree, no summary of its source — and the fork becomes real when its first message lands; until then it is a draft that knows its origin. What its first turn carries from the source is resolved then, under the provider that turn runs under: natively where the provider can fork its own conversation, otherwise rebuilt from the record — and either way the start is a fact of the record ([[Assistant]]).

## The thread space

Opening a thread lands on its space, which is **the adopted thread view whole** ([[T3code Thread View]]; amended 2026-09-03): the header with the project leading and the thread's title after it, the conversation, the [[Composer]], and the terminal drawer, with the thread's own views opening as [[Thread Tabs|thread tabs]] beside it in a split (2026-09-24; formerly a right panel beside them as a full-height column). What Mercurian adds is enumerated, and nothing else differs: the **[[Checkpoint Graph|Checkpoints]]** view among the thread tabs; a repository switcher in the header; **Fork here** on every message of yours, in place of the adopted view's destructive revert — a new thread at that message's checkpoint with your words back in the composer, the thread you came from untouched (the edit-and-branch move of [[Composer]] under its product name); the branch banner and the [[Memory|memory]] overlays; and beneath, the runtime: every send is an ordinary turn on the thread, and its history is recorded from the turn. There is no mode to enter or leave: one composer sends every kind of turn, and the only additional control is the **runtime mode**, which governs working-tree actions and nothing else — a turn that touches no worktree never meets it ([[Assistant]]). The adopted view's own plan mode stays off behind a legacy setting and never surfaces: Mercurian has no plan mode (the ruling on [[Mercurian]]). _(Superseded by this: the 2026-08-28 reading of the space as a Mercurian pane with three corner views, and the code view behind one of them.)_

**The conversation is the thread's own turns (2026-09-24; formerly "the conversation follows the selected path", 2026-09-05).** Older messages load in small pages in the same timeline. Selecting a point in [[Checkpoint Graph|Checkpoints]] shows the conversation through that point; selecting the tip restores the full conversation. **Back to latest** appears only while viewing an earlier checkpoint, alongside **Fork from here** to continue in a new thread. Inspection preserves the current draft and does not rewind the working tree. **Fork here** remains available on earlier user messages. What a forked thread shows of its parent's conversation is reopened below.

## The working state

A thread that builds carries working state — all of it thread-scoped, none of it shared until a human act ships it:

- **Threads own branches and checkpoints; the project owns the worktrees.** Every thread opens its branch eagerly — a system-prefixed ref in each repository the thread's project links, cheap, local, never pushed by the system — so no machinery ever has to guess whether a turn will edit. The working trees themselves are a small **pool of slots per project** (default three, adjustable in [[Settings]]), shared by every thread in the project. A slot is one directory holding a worktree of **every** repository the project links, arranged at the repositories' own relative positions on disk — a repository that sits beside its sibling in your checkouts sits beside it in every slot, so paths that reach across repositories keep resolving — and it materializes whole: no turn ever sees some of the project's repositories without the others. A turn claims a free slot, switches it to the thread's branches, restores the thread's checkpoint state, and runs — which is also what grounds its reads in the thread's own built state. A claim never waits on a busy slot: another free slot is taken, or a new one materializes up to the pool's cap — one claim per turn, so there is nothing left to deadlock — and at the cap the turn waits for a slot, visibly ([[Composer]]); steering a turn already running never waits. Slot reuse is the economy: installed dependencies and build state survive across threads, which keeps working state affordable for enormous repositories and for stacks with no shared package store. A new thread inherits no working state of its own — a fork's branches start at the checkpoint it forked from, where its parent built; a thread born blank starts from the base ref, which defaults per repository from [[Settings]]. Gitignored files are machine state riding the slot, never the line — the familiar semantics of switching branches in your own checkout; a thread that needs pristine ignored state needs a fresh slot.
- **Every turn ends in a snapshot; the thread's branch carries only deliberate commits.** Each thread keeps two records in every repository the slot holds. The **thread's branch** is the one you and the agent see: it moves only when a person or an agent commits, and the product never writes to it on its own. Beside it, hidden from every branch listing and never pushed, runs the thread's **snapshot chain**: at the end of every turn the runtime captures the whole working tree — tracked and untracked, ignored files excluded — as one snapshot, whether or not the agent committed, and links it to the snapshot before it. A snapshot is the **whole tree**, never a delta and never a reference to the branch's content: restoring it needs nothing but the snapshot itself. Each snapshot also records where the thread's branch stood when it was taken — a pointer to that exact commit, which keeps it alive however the branch is later rewritten — so the branch commits a turn made — none, one, or several — are read straight from the chain, and a rewrite or a departure (below) is derived, never guessed at. A snapshot carries its kind: **settled** when the turn ended well; **partial** when it crashed or you cancelled it — marked as partial wherever checkpoints show, the next message on the line continuing from it; **recovery** when a slot was found dirty with no turn attached; and **external** when a turn's opening capture found the tree changed since the last snapshot — a person editing in the slot, a pull, a script — because a turn opens with a capture too, written only when something differs. Agent diligence is not the invariant; the snapshot is. (Working names while this was designed: the snapshot branch and the user branch.) A slot never switches without capturing: a slot dirty but idle is snapshotted-then-switched, and nothing is ever discarded by reuse — the stash stack is never used. Attached surfaces — a running turn, an open terminal, a preview, an external editor — hold their slot, and a held slot is simply busy: switched by no one. (Held slots are also locked against worktree removal, so no cleanup — the product's or a stray command's — can take an active slot.)
- **The branch is the agent's to move; the thread's ref is the product's to keep.** Inside a turn the agent may commit, amend, rebase, or reset on the thread's branch — that is just what a branch is, and the chain records the rewrite as plainly as it records a commit. What the product keeps is the _ref_: every thread owns a branch in the product's own namespace, and a turn's snapshot notes when the working tree ended somewhere else — on another branch, a new one, or no branch at all. A **departed** turn changes nothing on the thread's branch; its checkpoint says where the tree went; the next turn on the thread returns the slot to the thread's branch with the last snapshot's files restored over it, so the work survives as uncommitted changes on the thread's own branch, and the departed commits stay reachable in git for a person to recover. The product itself never commits to a ref outside its namespace and never pushes from a slot but through the exits below; a branch checked out in your own working copy cannot be checked out in a slot at all — git's own guard, kept. What a snapshot does not hold: staged-versus-unstaged state (a restored slot has everything unstaged, the familiar post-checkout state), gitignored files (machine state riding the slot), and anything written outside the working tree.
- **You may edit while a turn runs, and the turn is told.** The slot is the agent's working tree and yours at once: editing a file in it during a turn — in the [[Atlas Editor|editor]] or any other way — is delivered to the running turn as a steer saying what changed, batched at a natural pause rather than per keystroke, shown in the [[Composer]] as sent, and holdable: you can keep edits back until you choose to release them. Where the provider takes a steer in place the turn continues; where it cannot, the turn is interrupted and restarted with the edit in hand, its earlier attempt kept beneath it ([[Checkpoint Graph]]). Nothing is ever delivered to a turn that has ended — an edit outside a turn summons no one, and your next message carries it ([[Assistant]], [[Memory]]). The turn's checkpoint records the steer, and its snapshot holds your edits beside the agent's; whose change is whose is the open decision below. _(2026-09-24.)_
- Moving your position to an earlier checkpoint **restores** that thread's working state to the checkpoint — the graph is the rewind, and it destroys nothing; the destructive revert the coding session carried is retired, its job done non-destructively by fork-and-restore. Viewing a checkpoint needs no worktree at all — its files and diffs read straight from the snapshot chain, in any number of windows at once; the physical restore happens when a turn next claims a slot for the line. Continuing from an earlier checkpoint opens a new thread — a fork — whose branch starts at the commit that checkpoint's snapshot recorded and whose slot receives the snapshot's files over it — so **no thread's branch ever moves backwards**, and every rewind is a fork. Moving in a thread whose turn is still running is refused; the fork is the way around.
- **Memory rides the thread like any other repository.** A project's memory repository is a member of every slot; the thread's branch and snapshot chain cover it exactly as they cover code, and what any thread sees of the memory derives from its ancestry because its branch does ([[Memory]], "Branches, and the merge home"). _(2026-09-01: formerly a branch of the thread's, opened by the first amendment — re-scoped to the line with the snapshot chain.)_
- **Three exits, all human.** Code leaves by git actions from the code view — push and a pull request per repository. The thread's branch carries only what you or the agent committed, so the code view shows beside it what the latest snapshot holds beyond the branch — files changed and never committed — and the exit **offers to commit them first**, as one commit you name; declining ships the branch as it stands. Memory leaves by the recommended merge home, the same exit shaped for a repository that may never leave the machine; the record leaves by [[Publishing|publishing]]. "Forks and merges are human-driven only" ([[DAG]]) holds at every layer, including these.

Teardown keeps the floor adopted from [[T3code Worktree Lifecycle]] (resolved 2026-07 for the coding sessions that preceded the unification): never on an undoable act, never unconfirmed, branches that carry commits always survive; delete — private-only — tears down, and archiving offers cleanup without ever performing it. Under the pool, teardown is the pool shrinking — slots removed with the floor intact — plus one new candidate the eager branches create: a branch whose thread never built and whose work no thread took in points at nothing anyone needs, and may be pruned under the same confirmation — its snapshot chain with it, since a chain keeps every tree it ever captured until it is pruned. A thread whose work another thread has taken in ([[Merges]]) and that has stopped is such a candidate — its committed branch surviving — offered, never performed.

**Refs are the truth; the recorded name follows them.** Everything the chain keeps — snapshots, the pointer to where the branch stood, the partial mark — is held by commit, never by name, so renaming or even deleting a thread's branch by hand cannot reach it. What a hand rename breaks is the product's memory of the name, which the next claim would check out. So the record follows the ref: at each capture the runtime already takes, a turn opening and a turn settling, the checked-out branch is compared with the recorded one — the same commit under a different name is a rename, and the record adopts the name; a different commit is a departure, handled as above. A branch missing at claim is the one case that needs a person: the product offers to recreate it at the commit the thread's last snapshot recorded. The product's own rename renames the thread's branch in every repository at once — one name per thread — while a hand rename in one repository is adopted there alone and reported, never corrected. A fork always opens fresh generated names, the thread's title with the new thread's own token, and never renames its ancestor, whose branch may already be pushed. One phantom to prevent: re-pointing an unbuilt branch by a recorded name that no longer exists would create an empty branch under the old name, so a re-point checks that the name still exists before forcing it (resolved under "built," below).

**Amended (2026-08-28): working state re-scopes from the thread to the line.** As first unified, a thread owned one worktree per repository, restore-on-move ranged across all lines, and two lines running code turns against the same repository met a refusal. Allowing agents to run on several branches at once dissolved that: the refusal is retired, replaced by per-line isolation. What thread scoping bought — one working copy to reason about — lazy materialization keeps affordable: a line pays for working state only when a turn actually edits a repository, and during planning none does. Given up: restore reaching across lines (restore is now line-local; another line's state is that line's business), and the single-worktree simplicity, spent deliberately on parallel building.

**Amended (2026-08-31): the pool replaces per-line worktrees; the runtime commits.** Three days after working state moved to the line, the directory ownership dissolved entirely: lines own branches and checkpoints — cheap refs and snapshots — and the working trees are a capped pool of interchangeable slots per repository, as recorded above. What forced it: per-line worktrees made disk unbounded — a checkout is the size of the repository, ignored codegen re-runs per copy, and shared package stores are a luxury many stacks don't have — while the pool makes slot count the one knob a heavy repository turns down. Settled in the same pass: line branches are eager (predicting whether a turn will edit — and memory made every turn a candidate — was more machinery than the refs cost); the turn's settled checkpoint is a real commit the runtime makes on the line's branch, no longer only hidden bookkeeping, with **partial checkpoints** for turns that end badly; and claims never wait on a specific slot, which is what makes multi-repository turns deadlock-free by construction. Given up: "most lines never touch disk" — every turn claims a slot now, and what stays lazy is the pool's growth; per-line isolation of ignored state, shared by design inside a pool; and the guarantee that a turn can always start at once — at the cap, a send waits for a slot, and says so.

**Amended (2026-09-01): the runtime snapshots; only people and agents commit.** The 2026-08-31 pass had the runtime end every settled turn with a commit of its own on the line's branch. Designing memory's branch against it surfaced the cost: a branch a person will push and a pull request will show was accumulating commits nobody made — sweeps of whatever the turn left behind, unrelated changes included — and curating them out was being asked of every exit. The split above answers it: the snapshot chain takes over everything the harness commit was for — the durable, forkable record of each turn's tree, real ancestry between turns, the changed-files reading — in a namespace no branch listing shows and nothing pushes, while the line's branch is left to mean exactly what a branch means in your own checkout. What the harness commit also bought, a branch that always carries the work, moves to the exit: pushing offers to commit what the chain holds beyond the branch, once, named by you. Settled in the same pass: a snapshot records where the branch stood, so the branch's movement during a turn is derived from the chain rather than tracked beside it; a turn's opening capture, written only on a difference, gives outside changes a snapshot kind of their own; departed turns leave the line's ref alone and say where the tree went; and memory's branch re-scopes from the thread to the line, so a fork reads the memory as its ancestor left it by construction. Given up: a branch that is always up to date with the work — an agent that never commits leaves a branch with nothing on it until the exit; staged state, which no snapshot keeps; and the product's freedom to tidy a line's branch, which it now never touches. A fold ([[Merges]]) is unchanged in kind: a real merge of the lines' branches by the responding turn, the carrying line's chain continuing with its next snapshot recording the merge commit — snapshots never merge one another; the shape of history lives in the branches and in the plan's own graph.

**Amended (2026-08-31, later): the slot re-scopes from the repository to the project.** Same day, the pool's unit moved. As first recorded, a slot was one repository's working tree, pooled and claimed per repository; now a slot belongs to the project and holds all of its repositories at once, arranged as they sit on your disk. What forced it: a project's repositories are not independent — builds and tooling routinely reach across them by relative path, so working trees materialized one repository at a time, each wherever its own pool happened to put it, hand the agent a world where those paths resolve to nothing; and a turn editing one repository beside stale neighbors is grounding in a fiction. The project slot answers both: every claim yields the whole project, whole — the arrangement preserved so cross-repository paths keep resolving, and no repository materialized without the others. It also retires what the per-repository design had to prove: deadlock-freedom mattered because a multi-repository turn held several claims, and one claim per turn leaves nothing to deadlock. Given up: the per-repository knob — the slot count is the project's now, and every slot carries the project's largest repository; and slot sharing across projects — a repository linked by two projects is checked out in each project's pool, no longer one economy. Both costs land on the heaviest setups, and the knob still turns down.

## History: one line per thread

**Amended (2026-09-24): a thread is one line; the branching history moves to the [[DAG]] of threads.** From 2026-07 the design carried one branching, merging history _inside_ each thread — the Commit Tree — where lines forked apart and merged back as commits with two parents, and everything else (working state, memory branches, the model choice, drafts) was scoped to the line. The orchestrator this product now sits on ([[Strategy]]) models a thread as exactly one line: a counted sequence of turns, each ending in a checkpoint, with forks creating new threads that remember their lineage, and with context moving between threads as an explicit, inspectable transfer rather than a merge commit. Rather than carry a second history model over the adopted one, the design adopts it: what was a _line_ is now a _thread_, and every rule written for lines transfers unchanged — the branch and snapshot chain, the eager branch, the model choice as a fact of the record, drafts riding the thread, replies in different threads running at once. What moves is only where the shape lives: fork points and folds are edges of the DAG between threads, never commits within one. Given up: one history per thread as the container of its forks — a thread's forks are sibling threads in the [[Left Sidebar]] and the DAG, not paths inside it; and the merge as a commit with two parents — a fold is context taken into the taking thread's next turn ([[Merges]]). Kept whole: forks and merges are human-driven only; nothing is ever destroyed; every rewind is a fork; the working state, snapshot chain, and exits above. The Commit Tree note is deleted; git holds it.

## Reconciling against t3code's thread

The shell this design adapts drives all work through a thread and its screen ([[T3code Thread]], [[T3code Thread View]]); with the unification, Mercurian's reconciliation simplifies to three standing departures. **History:** the adopted orchestrator forks a thread into a new thread with its lineage recorded, takes a fork's context back, and still rewinds a thread in place by marking what came after as rolled back ([[T3code Thread]]); Mercurian takes the fork and the transfer whole and keeps rewind as a fork — every return to an earlier checkpoint opens a new thread with its worktree restored, nothing is marked rolled back, and the revert control is ours under the name **Fork here** (2026-09-03; re-read 2026-09-24). **Plan mode:** t3code's plan is a turn's proposal approved by clicking implement; Mercurian has no plan mode, no plan artifact, and no implement moment — a turn that should change code changes code, and the conversation's own steps are the checkpoints (the ruling on [[Mercurian]]). **The record:** a thread's turns continue one live session, as the adopted thread's do; what differs is only what the record keeps of a fork's start — carried natively or rebuilt from the record, it is a fact of the record rather than internal to a session _(2026-09-03; formerly every reply rebuilt from the record, and compaction rode each reply's recorded reconstruction boundary — [[Assistant]])_.

## What exists today

Built: the thread's conversation and history as designed here, minus the code view; the working-state pool — project slots, eager thread branches — and, since 2026-09-02, the snapshot chain: every slot-backed turn ends in a chained snapshot of its kind (settled, partial, recovery, external), the thread's branch moves only when a person or agent commits, a departed turn is marked and returned to its branch on the next claim, a fork lays its ancestor's snapshot over its branch, and the diff panel reads the thread's uncommitted delta from the chain rather than from a directory. The exit's offer to commit is the existing git control on a dirty tree. Since 2026-09-03, building spans the project: the implement gate is gone — starting to build from any checkpoint opens the session directly, asking only for a runtime mode and a model — and a session's turns may edit every repository the project links. Each repository gets its own snapshot on the chain at every turn end, is built on its own, keeps its own branch facts and pull request on the session's record, and shows its changed files as its own group on the turn; a hand rename is adopted in the repository it happened in. The session stands in one home repository, the first the project links (the ordered list is not yet draggable, and the standing cannot yet be changed per thread), with the rest granted to a provider that reaches several roots and named as out of reach otherwise; the session header acts on one repository at a time through a switcher. Since 2026-09-05, a turn's snapshot is taken when the turn ends whichever provider runs it: a provider that reports its diff mid-turn no longer settles the turn early, so an agent's last-minute commits land on its own turn's record rather than as changes outside a turn. Not built: the thread space as the adopted view — its pinned Checkpoints, the switcher in its header, Fork here — and thread vocabulary in the product; the built space is still the Mercurian pane with three corner views. Built and now retired by the ruling: plan and spec revisions, the artifact pane, and issue import, whose removal from the code is work the ruling implies. The adopted orchestrator (2026-09-24, [[Strategy]]) carries the thread-as-one-line model this note now follows — forks as new threads with recorded lineage, resolved at the fork's first message, and context taken from a fork back into its source. How Mercurian's working-state pool and snapshot chain sit on it is not yet built or verified.

## Open Decisions

### What does a forked thread's conversation show?

The adopted timeline is one line's turns, so a fork opens on a conversation that begins at the fork; the thread's earlier turns are one step away in Checkpoints, but the conversation itself does not say where it came from. Two readings. **Inherit the ancestry:** the turns leading to the fork point appear above the line's own — folded, read-only, visibly not this line's, loaded on demand rather than whole — so a fork reads as a continuation. **Mark the origin only:** the conversation stays exactly the adopted timeline, with a seam at its top naming the checkpoint it forked from and opening it. Inheriting is the truer reading of "one thread, one history" and keeps Fork here usable on an ancestor's message without leaving the space; marking keeps the timeline identical to the adopted one and asks nothing of it. Either way there is one timeline implementation — whichever reading wins is composed from the adopted entries, never a second renderer — and artifact revisions inline are moot now that there are none. **Recommendation:** inherit the ancestry, folded and on demand, with the seam named where the line's own turns begin — a fork that reads as a fresh start contradicts what a fork is.

**Superseded (2026-09-05): mark the origin.** A fork names its starting checkpoint above its conversation, even before its first message and after work continues. Opening that origin brings up Checkpoints at that position without changing the current line or its draft; Return to latest ends the inspection. Earlier turns remain readable through Checkpoints. This chooses the smaller of the two readings: a visible connection to the earlier history while preserving the familiar conversation. Given up: scrolling directly into ancestor messages and using Fork here on them inside the conversation; those acts stay in Checkpoints. No ancestry is loaded merely to show the marker.

**Superseded (2026-09-05): inherit the ancestry in a separate section.** A signpost alone leaves the earlier conversation missing. Show the actual messages above the fork’s own, folded and read-only, with a clear boundary and small pages on demand. Keep the ordinary message appearance and Fork here on earlier user messages. Given up: an entirely line-local conversation; the visible continuity is the point of a fork. This supersedes the origin-only choice above, including its Return to latest action.

**Resolved (2026-09-05): one continuous conversation along the selected path.** A separate folded section still makes inherited messages feel unlike the rest of the thread. Show them in the normal timeline with no seam. The selected checkpoint determines where that conversation ends, on every line. Returning to the tip restores later messages; a tip never offers a redundant return action. Continue from an earlier checkpoint by forking, preserving the original line. This supersedes both the origin marker and the folded-section choices above.

**Reopened (2026-09-24): a fork is a thread of its own.** The 2026-09-05 resolution read a fork's ancestry as part of one thread's history and showed one continuous conversation along the selected path. With a fork its own thread, its conversation is its own turns and the parent's turns are the parent's. The two readings return: **inherit** — the parent's conversation through the fork point shown above the fork's own, read-only — or **mark the origin** — a seam at the top naming the origin checkpoint and opening the parent thread there, with the [[DAG]] showing the family. **Recommendation:** mark the origin. A fork now has a parent one step away, and copying the parent's turns into the fork's timeline would make one conversation appear in two threads. Given up, if taken: reading the whole path without leaving the thread.

### What do archiving and deleting a thread mean?

_(Resolved 2026-07 on the plan note that preceded this one, when a plan was the unit of work; moved here when threads became the unit.)_ **Resolved: delete exists only while a thread is fully private; once anything is published, archive is the only disappearance.** A fully private thread was never seen by anyone else — deleting it leaves no trace, and re-importing its origin issue simply starts fresh. Once any commit is published, the thread belongs to shared history: it can be archived — navigational and reversible, restorable from the Archived page in [[Settings]], with re-importing the origin issue resurfacing the archived thread rather than duplicating it — but never deleted. The reasoning follows [[Publishing]]'s temperament: publish is the one deliberate crossing into shared history — before it the work is yours to destroy, after it it isn't only yours. Given up: the ability to fully remove a published mistake; the mitigation is archive. Alternatives declined: archive-only with no delete verb (abandoned two-message drafts pile up forever), and full t3code semantics (deleting published history contradicts [[Publishing]] and breaks import idempotency). A successor question — who a thread disappears for — is open on [[Settings]].

**Amended (2026-09-04; narrowed 2026-09-16):** a rule that disappearance leaves project plans and specs intact retired with those documents. What survives of it is general: cleanup never discards a thread's snapshots or branch while they hold the only copy of work, and never silently publishes or merges private work. _(2026-09-24: a fork is its own thread with its own lifecycle — archiving a parent leaves its forks where they are, and the [[DAG]] still shows the family.)_

### Which repository does the agent stand in?

_(Raised 2026-09-01, when sessions first spanned a project's repositories.)_ A slot arranges every repository as it sits on disk, but an agent still runs _from_ one directory: its working directory is where relative paths resolve first, where a bare shell opens, and what a provider that can only see one root sees at all. The design never says which repository that is. As **the first repository the project links**, the choice is deterministic, matches how grounding already works (first root is home, the rest are additional), and asks nothing of the user — but link order becomes load-bearing, and relinking repositories can silently change where later turns stand. As **a per-project choice** — a designated home repository, defaulting to the first link — the standing is a fact the user can see and set, at the cost of one more project setting. As **per turn**, chosen by the agent from the conversation's content, the home follows the work, at the cost of a wait and a guess. **Recommendation:** the first linked repository, with the [[Teams|project]]'s repository list presented as ordered — dragging a repository to the top _is_ choosing the home — so the fact stays visible without a setting of its own. What this gives up: a home that follows the work; a provider confined to one root sees the first repository whatever the work is about, which the narrowing notice must keep saying plainly.

**Resolved (2026-09-02): a per-thread choice, defaulting to the project's repository order.** The fork above was framed as _who_ decides — link order, a setting, the agent — when the scope was the real question. The [[Teams|project]]'s repository list is ordered and its first repository is the default home; a thread may stand in another, chosen at thread start beside its base-ref choice, and the choice is fixed once the first turn runs — a provider keeps its conversation against the directory it stood in, and moving the home under a running thread would start that conversation over. A thread standing anywhere but the project's default says so where it shows its provider, and every turn-opening commit records where it stood beside what it ran under ([[Assistant]]; the reachability question on [[Providers]]). It is one fact, not two: the thread's home is also the first root grounding reads from, so research and building in one thread agree about where home is. Lines inherit their thread's home — a fork that needs a different home is a new thread. Why per thread: it is what the declined per-turn option wanted — a home that follows the work — without the wait and the guess, chosen by the person rather than the agent; and it is the only shape that makes the narrowing notice actionable, since a provider confined to one root now sees the repository you chose rather than whichever was linked first. The precedent is the base ref, already a per-thread choice with a settings default ([[Settings]]). Given up: a home that moves freely mid-thread; the project-order-only reading, under which relinking could silently move where later turns stand; and the agent choosing per turn.

**Amended (2026-09-02, later): where a turn stands is derived per turn, and the choice moves between turns.** The per-thread home stays the default, but the resolution above stamped the standing onto the thread and fixed it after the first turn for a reason that does not hold: the product already gives every effective change of provider or model one meaning — "the next reply starts a fresh assistant session with the thread's recorded history carried into it" ([[Providers]]) — and the record is the product's, so a change of standing is the same act at the same cost, for building as for conversation. So: a provider that reaches several roots stands in the thread's home with every other repository and the memory granted beside it; a provider confined to one root stands at the **slot root** — the directory holding every repository as it sits on disk — unless the thread has chosen a repository for it to stand in, so that a picker flip to a confined provider never silently loses the rest of the project. The standing is changeable between turns like the model picker, and every turn-opening commit records where the turn stood and what it reached beside what it ran under ([[Assistant]]; the reachability question on [[Providers]]). Before sending, the [[Composer|composer]] states what the next turn will not have, in the place it already states a signed-out provider: the designated memory, when the standing does not contain it; reads across repositories, for a confined provider standing in one, with the slot root named as the fix; and, at the slot root, that the repositories' own agent configuration may not load (open on [[Providers]]). No warning announces the fresh session itself — that is the standing rule for any change, not an event — and its size is what the reconstruction reading on [[Composer]] already shows. The snapshot chain is untouched by any of this: a snapshot is a whole tree per repository and knows nothing of where the agent stood; the standing is a fact of the turn and lives in the history. Given up: fixed-after-first-turn simplicity, and any promise of provider-side continuity across a change, which the product never made for any change.

_(2026-09-24: "lines inherit their thread's home — a fork that needs a different home is a new thread" resolves itself: a fork *is* a new thread, and chooses its home at birth like any thread, defaulting to its parent's.)_

### Whose change is it, inside a steered turn?

_(Raised 2026-09-24, with editing alongside a running turn.)_ A turn's snapshot is the whole tree, and its effects — memory updated, code changed — derive from the record without reading anyone's prose. Once you can edit while the turn runs, the snapshot holds your edits beside the agent's, and "code changed" no longer says who changed it. As **the editor's record**, the two-peer document knows which edits were yours, and the checkpoint marks those files as yours, exact for everything the editor saw; an edit made in the slot by some other tool is not seen and reads as the turn's. As **the turn's, with a mark**, everything in the snapshot is the turn's and the checkpoint only says it was steered by your edit, with the steer's text in the popover: honest and coarse. As **a snapshot of your own**, your edits are captured as an external snapshot before the agent's next write lands, so the chain itself separates the hands: exact, at the cost of snapshots landing mid-turn, which the chain was never designed to do. **Recommendation:** the editor's record, with the mark as the floor — files the editor saw are marked yours, the checkpoint says it was steered and what it was told, and an edit the editor did not see reads as the turn's, which the base teaching tells the agent to expect. Given up: exactness for edits made outside the editor.

### What does "built" mean for a repository the line never touched?

_(Raised 2026-09-01.)_ The line's branches are re-pointable "until built" — a base-ref change before the first build moves them — and a line that "never built and never merged" is a teardown candidate. Both rules were written for a line that touches one repository. Under project slots a settled turn touches _some_ of the line's repositories: the touched branches carry commits and are pinned, but an untouched repository's branch has no commits and, read literally, is still "unbuilt" — re-pointable and prunable — even while a slot on that line has it checked out. As **a fact about the line**, one settled turn pins every repository's branch: simple, no branch ever moves under a checked-out worktree, and the line's branches share one fate; given up is pruning an untouched repository's empty branch, which now looks built. As **a fact about each repository**, only touched branches pin; untouched ones stay movable and prunable, and the machinery must add the one guard the literal reading lacks: never move a branch a slot currently has checked out. **Recommendation:** per repository, with that guard — it keeps "a branch whose line never built points at nothing anyone needs" true at the granularity teardown actually prunes, and the guard is the same rule a person's own checkout already enforces (you cannot force-move the branch you are standing on). Given up: the tidiness of one fate per line; a built line can still carry empty, movable branches in the repositories it never reached.

**Resolved (2026-09-02): per repository, with the guard — and "touched" is read from the chain, never from the branch.** The entry's premise predates the same-day amendment that stopped the runtime committing: touched branches usually carry nothing, because only people and agents commit. So a repository counts as **touched**, and its branch as **built**, when the line's snapshot chain holds a snapshot of that repository whose tree differs from the branch's base, or when the branch itself has moved; a settled turn that captured a repository unchanged leaves it untouched, which is what keeps this reading from collapsing into one fate per line. Two guards: a branch a slot currently has checked out never moves, and a re-point checks that the branch still exists under its recorded name before forcing it — forcing a missing name would create a fresh, empty branch under it, the phantom described under "The working state." Given up, as recommended: one fate per line.

### What does a partial checkpoint record across repositories?

_(Raised 2026-09-01.)_ A turn that ends badly leaves a partial checkpoint "marked as partial wherever checkpoints show," and every repository it dirtied is snapshotted so the next message continues from all of them. But what the partial's own _record_ shows is undecided: a settled turn's record lists changed files per repository; a partial's could show the same, or only the home repository's files, or only the mark. **Recommendation:** the same record as a settled turn — files per repository, plus the mark — because the reason a partial exists is that someone will read it to decide what to do next, and a record that hides two of three repositories' changes makes that decision blind. Given up: nothing but the work; the snapshots already exist per repository, so this is a matter of showing what is kept.

**Resolved (2026-09-02): the same record as a settled turn, plus the mark.** A partial checkpoint lists its changed files per repository exactly as a settled one does, with the partial mark wherever checkpoints show. The record exists so that someone can decide what to do next — continue, fork past it, abandon — and a record that hides what the turn left in any repository makes that decision blind. Given up: nothing but the rendering; the snapshots already exist per repository.

### What does a slot hold for a repository that is not one?

_(Raised 2026-09-02.)_ [[Repositories]] admits any folder — "git is expected but not demanded" — with everything working-tree-shaped lighting up only when the folder actually is a repository. A slot materializes the whole project as worktrees, and a folder with no git has no worktree to give: a project with such a member cannot claim a slot at all, so it cannot build. As **shared in place**, the slot reaches the folder where it sits, readable and writable, outside every line's branch and chain — every change external and unsnapshotted, which the turn's record must say plainly. As **copied**, the slot carries its own copy, whole and unversioned, at the cost of disk and of edits that never leave the slot. As **refused until made a repository**, building stays unavailable while grounding reads it regardless, and the product offers the one act that fixes it: initializing the folder as a repository. **Recommendation:** refused, with the offer. A folder outside the chain breaks "every turn ends in a snapshot," and the product should not carry a member it cannot keep. Given up: building against a folder someone deliberately keeps out of git.

**Resolved (2026-09-02): refused — at the door, not at the first build.** A [[Teams|project]] links only folders that are repositories; linking one that is not offers to initialize it, in place, and declining leaves it unlinked. The refusal moves from the build to the link because a member the chain cannot keep should never be a member, and because nothing else is simpler: a repository at the slot root cannot hold a member's files (a nested checkout is recorded as a pointer, never as files), gives the folder no branch, base, or exit, and only ever copies the person's folder with no way back; a product-owned shadow repository over the folder would work and was declined as more machinery than the case is worth. Given up: grounding a project in a folder someone keeps out of git. The [[Repositories]] list still admits any folder; a project takes only repositories.

### What happens to a turn's changes in ignored files?

_(Raised 2026-09-02.)_ A snapshot excludes ignored files by design: they are machine state that rides the slot, which is what keeps working state affordable. But an agent edits ignored files too — an environment file, a local configuration, generated output it regenerated — and a turn that does so leaves work the chain does not hold. When another line takes the slot, or the line's next turn lands in a different one, those changes are gone, silently, and the turn continues from a tree that is quietly wrong about them. Slot affinity — a line re-claims the slot it last held when that slot is free — makes the loss rare, not impossible. As **snapshotted too**, ignored files join the tree: exact, and ruinous, since the chain would then carry every dependency install and build output per turn, the very economy the pool exists for. As **kept by pattern**, a per-project list of ignored paths worth keeping is captured with the tree despite the ignore and restored on claim: exact for what it names, at the cost of a setting and of secrets entering the repository's object store under refs that are hidden and never pushed. As **reported**, the product records on the turn's checkpoint which ignored files the turn wrote — known exactly for the agent's own edits, which the product sees as tool calls and can check against the repository's ignore rules — and says they stay with the slot; nothing is captured, and the base teaching tells the agent the rule. **Recommendation:** reported, with affinity as it stands, and keeping by pattern deferred until a real case demands it. The invariant is honesty, not capture: a turn's record says what it kept and what it left behind, and an agent that must persist something ignored is taught to write it somewhere the chain keeps. Given up: exact reconstruction of ignored state; a person who relied on it is told in the record rather than surprised in the slot.

**Resolved (2026-09-02): reported, as recommended.** Ignored files stay the slot's. What changes is that a turn's checkpoint says which ignored files the turn wrote and that they did not travel with the line — exact for the agent's own edits, which the product sees as they happen and checks against the repository's ignore rules — and the base teaching tells the agent that anything it must keep goes where the chain keeps it. Slot affinity stands as the mitigation, not the guarantee. Declined: snapshotting ignored files, which would trade the pool's economy for exactness nobody asked for; deferred: keeping by pattern, until a real case names the file worth keeping. Given up: exact reconstruction of ignored state.
